# Harmonizing Melodies on the Lyre

A practical guide to adding chords to melodies on 7 and 8 string lyres tuned to ancient Greek modes.

---

## Core Principle: Chord Tones

The simplest rule for harmonization: **choose a chord that contains the melody note**.

When the melody plays E, you can use:
- **C major** (C E G) - E is the 3rd
- **E minor** (E G B) - E is the root
- **A minor** (A C E) - E is the 5th

Each choice creates a different mood and color.

---

## Chord Selection Guidelines

### 1. Strong Beats Get Priority

Harmonize the notes on strong beats (beat 1 and 3 in 4/4 time, beat 1 in 3/4). Weak beats can be passing tones that don't need to match the chord.

```
Melody:  C  D  E  F | G  A  G  E |
Beat:    1  2  3  4 | 1  2  3  4 |
Chord:   C          | G     C    |
```

D and F are passing tones here - they don't match C major but resolve quickly.

### 2. Chord Tone Hierarchy

When a melody note belongs to multiple chords, prefer:

1. **Chords where the melody is the root** - strongest, most stable
2. **Chords where the melody is the 5th** - strong, supportive
3. **Chords where the melody is the 3rd** - colorful, can feel unresolved

### 3. Phrase Endings

End phrases on stable chords. In modal music, the "home" chord is typically:

| Mode         | Home Chord | Secondary Rest |
|--------------|------------|----------------|
| Mixolydian   | B dim*     | E minor        |
| Lydian       | C major    | A minor        |
| Phrygian     | D minor    | F major        |
| Dorian       | E minor    | G major        |
| Hypolydian   | F major    | A minor        |
| Hypophrygian | G major    | C major        |
| Hypodorian   | A minor    | E minor        |

*Mixolydian's diminished tonic is unstable; E minor often substitutes.

## Chord Progression Patterns

### Simple Two-Chord Patterns

Many melodies work with just two alternating chords:

| Mode         | Common Pair       |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Lydian       | C major - G major |
| Phrygian     | D minor - A minor |
| Dorian       | E minor - A minor |
| Hypolydian   | F major - C major |
| Hypophrygian | G major - C major |
| Hypodorian   | A minor - D minor |

### The I-IV-V Equivalent

Modal music doesn't follow major/minor key conventions, but similar functions exist:

- **Tonic function**: The home chord (rest, resolution)
- **Subdominant function**: Creates gentle motion away from home
- **Dominant function**: Creates tension that wants to resolve home

In Hypophrygian (G A B C D E F):
- Tonic: G major
- Subdominant: C major
- Dominant: D minor

## When to Change Chords

### Change on:
- Strong beats when the melody note doesn't fit the current chord
- Phrase boundaries
- Significant melodic leaps
- Repeated notes (optional - adds interest)

### Hold when:
- Melody notes are passing tones moving quickly
- The melody is outlining the current chord (arpeggiated)
- Stepwise motion within the chord's scale

### Rate of Change

- **Slow harmonic rhythm**: One chord per measure - stately, calm
- **Medium**: Two chords per measure - balanced, natural
- **Fast**: One chord per beat - energetic, complex

For lyre accompaniment, slower is usually better. One or two chords per measure is typical.

---

## Practical Lyre Techniques

### Block Chords
Play all three chord notes simultaneously. Works well at phrase beginnings and endings.

### Broken Chords (Arpeggios)
Play chord notes in sequence (low to high or high to low). Creates flowing accompaniment.

### Drone + Melody
Hold or repeat the root note while playing the melody. Ancient and effective.

### Alternating Bass
Alternate between the root and fifth of the chord while the melody plays above.

## Handling Non-Chord Tones

Not every melody note needs to be in the chord. These "non-chord tones" add interest:

| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| Passing tone | Steps between chord tones | C-**D**-E over C major |
| Neighbor tone | Steps away and back | E-**F**-E over C major |
| Suspension | Held from previous chord | **D** resolving to C over C major |

**Rule**: Non-chord tones should be short and resolve to chord tones by step.

## Mode-Specific Tips

### Lydian (C D E F G A B)
The raised 4th (B) gives a bright, uplifting quality. Emphasize C major and G major.

### Phrygian (D E F G A B C)
The half-step from D to E creates a distinctive dark, Spanish-like color. Emphasize D minor.

### Dorian (E F G A B C D)
Has a serious but not dark quality. The E minor and A minor pairing is very effective.

### Hypolydian (F G A B C D E)
Bright and open. F major to C major movement is strong.

### Hypophrygian (G A B C D E F)
Similar to modern Mixolydian. G major to C major is the primary motion.

### Hypodorian (A B C D E F G)
Natural minor feel. A minor to D minor or A minor to E minor are primary movements.

## Step-by-Step Harmonization Process

1. **Identify the mode** - What notes does the melody use?

2. **Mark the strong beats** - These are your primary harmonization targets.

3. **Find phrase boundaries** - Where does the melody breathe or pause?

4. **Choose ending chords first** - Phrase endings should feel resolved.

5. **Work backwards** - Fill in chords that lead smoothly to your chosen endings.

6. **Simplify** - If in doubt, use fewer chord changes. Two or three chords per phrase is often enough.

## Example: Harmonizing a Simple Melody

Melody in Hypophrygian (G A B C D E F):
```
G  A  B  C | D  E  D  C | B  A  G  - |
```

**Step 1**: Strong beats are G, D, B (first beat of each measure)

**Step 2**: Phrase ends on G (measure 3)

**Step 3**: Work out chords:
- Measure 1: G is in G major (root) - use G major
- Measure 2: D is in G major (5th) or D minor (root) - G major works, or try D minor for variety
- Measure 3: B is in G major (3rd), phrase ends on G - use G major

**Result**:
```
Melody: G  A  B  C | D  E  D  C | B  A  G  - |
Chord:  G major   | D minor    | G major    |
```

Or simpler:
```
Melody: G  A  B  C | D  E  D  C | B  A  G  - |
Chord:  G major   | G major    | G major    |
```

Both work. The first has more harmonic motion; the second is more stable.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Too many chord changes** - Let the melody breathe. Not every note needs a new chord.

2. **Ignoring phrase structure** - Chord changes should support, not fight, the melody's natural shape.

3. **Always using root position** - Inversions (see CHORDS_BY_MODE.md) add variety and can create smoother motion.

4. **Forgetting the mode's character** - Each mode has a home note. Return to it.

5. **Over-harmonizing** - Sometimes a drone or single chord is more effective than constant movement.
